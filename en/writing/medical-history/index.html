Who knows what to ask

Written on 28 Sep 2026.

Every time someone asks me if I am not afraid of AI replacing me, I think of the anamnesis, the medical history interview.

In medical school, the first thing we learn is not diagnosis, it is questions. Anamnesis is a structure of directed questions that builds a patient's context before any decision. There is a maxim in medicine that sums it up: "clinical judgment is sovereign". It is not the test that decides alone; it is the reasoning built on the history, the context and the relevant facts of that specific person.

I spent years training this flow on shifts, in emergencies, in routine visits. Today, studying artificial intelligence and project management, I see that this way of thinking goes far beyond caring for someone. A good structure of questions solves big problems, not only individual health problems. That is how I plan my career and my projects: facing a problem means facing the facts that matter to solve it. A good analysis produces relevant data. Without it, there is no good decision.

That is where AI comes in, in my view. Much information that people can already gather, but that takes a lot of time and a big team, an AI tool can process in minutes. Electronic records in major hospitals already do this: models predicting high sepsis risk, models flagging patients who keep coming back to the emergency room. This is data that, in the end, helps direct someone's care.

But raw data is not a decision. AI will never replace the critical eye that only a person brings to a specific problem. It helps us reach the objective part of the history faster. Judging what it means is still, and I think will long remain, our job.

That is why I am not afraid. The question was never "who does it faster". It was always "who knows what to ask".
