CFM Normas

An open, structured repository of the historical archive of the Brazilian Federal Council of Medicine, from 1957 to 2026: norms in Markdown, with the validity status of each one, for physicians, researchers, developers, lawyers and AI agents.

Purpose

The project exists, first of all, to give the public an open, reliable archive of the CFM's norms.

Historically, the medical profession faces a great scatter of information sources, which often makes it hard for health professionals to align and to share regulatory knowledge quickly.

I wanted to share that knowledge and bring the medical community, researchers, developers and lawyers closer around an accessible, reliable base. There are 3,856 norms, from 1957 to 2026, in an open format and free to use. Historical coverage is complete for resolutions, opinions and recommendations; the archive keeps being updated every week.

The structure was designed for two readers: health professionals, and artificial intelligence agents that check ethical, regulatory and technical compliance in the practice of medicine.

Building the skills listed in the case card was the second purpose, and it comes with the first: the archive is the product, and the infrastructure shows how I think about a data product.

Problem

  • Scattered sources. Ethics norms, opinions on new procedures and rules on advertising and telemedicine sit across slow systems and loose PDFs.
  • Risk of citing a revoked norm. An AI agent that browses the web without a structured source may cite a norm that has already been revoked or changed. In medicine, following a norm that is no longer in force can lead to an ethics violation. That was the risk I wanted to reduce.
  • A portal that machines read poorly. The official portal shows PDFs inside a viewer, which makes them hard for AI agents and semantic-search pipelines to read without heavy scraping.

What I did

  • Archive: 3,856 CFM norms from 1957 to 2026 (2,458 resolutions, 1,384 opinions and 14 recommendations), each one in Markdown with a standard YAML header. Every year in the period has at least one norm.
  • Validity over time: each norm carries its status (in force, revoked or suspended). On 4 Nov 2026: 3,378 in force, 475 revoked and 3 suspended. Of the revoked ones, 467 point to the norm that changed them (the alterada_por_ids field).
  • Themes: 5 macro-themes (such as medical ethics and digital health) and 17 tags (such as telemedicine, electronic health record and artificial intelligence).
  • AI and data track: a separate catalog with 166 norms that touch on artificial intelligence, data protection and telemedicine. Four flag AI, 128 flag data protection and 54 flag telemedicine; a norm can sit in more than one group. The track includes CFM Resolution 2,454/2026 (use of artificial intelligence in medicine), 2,314/2022 (telemedicine) and the robotic surgery norms.
  • Lookup by AI agents: the JSON catalogs carry an index by theme and by tag. The agent asks for a tag, gets a list of identifiers and reads each norm straight from its URL, with no vector database and without downloading the repository. The full catalog is 4.7 MB; the AI track is 217 KB.
  • Governance: a JSON Schema (Draft-07) describes each norm, and each one stores the SHA-256 checksum of its content. Open CC BY 4.0 license.
  • Automatic update: a GitHub Actions workflow runs every Sunday at 01:00 (Brasília time), queries the current year on the CFM portal and uses the checksum manifest to rewrite only what changed. Then it regenerates the catalogs, the dashboard and the README metrics card. It has had successful runs on 25 Oct and 1 Nov 2026.

Decisions

  • The Federal Council, not the 27 regional ones. I chose the CFM, which issues the norms with national reach, and avoided maintaining 27 state portals that differ from one another.
  • The portal's own metadata. I found that the portal already delivers the metadata as JSON inside the page. I used that JSON instead of scraping HTML tables, which break easily.
  • No stored PDFs. Each PDF is read in memory (io.BytesIO) and converted straight to Markdown. The repository has no PDF file at all.
  • An update that only rewrites what changed. The manifest stores each norm's checksum; running the workflow again produces no change if nothing changed on the portal.
  • Collection by decade. I built the archive in five phases, from newest to oldest: 2020 to 2026, 2010 to 2019, 2000 to 2009, 1990 to 1999 and 1957 to 1989.
  • A dashboard that opens with two clicks. The dashboard data is embedded in the file itself, so it opens straight in the browser, with no server.

Results

Measured on 4 Nov 2026:

  • 3,856 norms over 70 years. Every resolution, opinion and recommendation from 1957 to 2026 that the portal lists.
  • 166 norms in the AI, data and telemedicine track, ready for an agent to query by theme.
  • 28 MB for the norms in Markdown, and 4.7 MB for the full catalog.
  • R$ 0.00 per month: hosting and automation on GitHub's free plan.

Limits

  • Not legal advice. The archive organizes public data and helps anchor an AI in official sources. It does not replace legal counsel or a regional council's opinion on a specific case.
  • Validity as of the last update. The status holds for 4 Nov 2026. For a decision with consequences, check the CFM portal.
  • "Complete" has a scope. Coverage is complete for resolutions, opinions and recommendations. Legal rulings are left for a later module, and the CFM's internal administrative ordinances were left out.
  • Links between norms go one way only. The revoga_ids field exists in the schema but is empty. Today the link goes from the revoked norm to the one that changed it, and not the other way around.
  • The AI track is a theme filter, built automatically. It points to where to look but does not say what each norm requires, and it may include norms that only touch the subject in passing. Only four flag artificial intelligence.
  • The weekly automation only looks at the current year. The historical loads were done separately.
